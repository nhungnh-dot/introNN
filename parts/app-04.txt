x_2+b\)<br><br>
            \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial b}=1\)
          </div>
        </div>
        <p class="small"><strong>Interpretation:</strong> changing \(w_1\) changes \(z\) in proportion to \(x_1\); changing \(w_2\) changes \(z\) in proportion to \(x_2\); changing the bias shifts \(z\) directly.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="card">
        <div class="h3">Pause and try these derivatives yourself</div>
        <ol class="small">
          <li>Starting from <span class="mono">\(L=(y-t)^2\)</span>, differentiate with respect to \(y\) before revealing the formula above.</li>
          <li>Starting from <span class="mono">z=\(w_1\)\(x_1\)+\(w_2\)\(x_2\)+b</span>, differentiate with respect to \(w_1\). Which terms are constants?</li>
          <li>Do the same for \(w_2\) and \(b\).</li>
          <li>For the sigmoid, use \(y=\sigma(z)\) and verify that \(\sigma^\prime(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\).</li>
        </ol>
      </div>

      <div class="card">
        <div class="h3">Collect the local derivatives</div>
        <p class="small">These are the pieces needed for backpropagation:</p>
        <div class="grid cards3 small">
          <div class="card"><div class="muted">Loss layer</div><div>\(L=(y-t)^2\)</div><div>\(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}=2(y-t)\)</div></div>
          <div class="card"><div class="muted">Activation layer</div><div class="mono">y=\(\sigma(z)\)</div><div>\(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}=y(1-y)\)</div></div>
          <div class="card"><div class="muted">Weighted-sum layer</div><div class="mono">z=\(w_1\)\(x_1\)+\(w_2\)\(x_2\)+b</div><div class="mono">\(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_1}=x_1\)<br>\(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_2}=x_2\)<br>\(\frac{\partial z}{\partial b}=1\)</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <details class="card">
        <summary>Step 2A checkpoint</summary>
        <div class="small">
          <p>1. In <span class="mono">\(L=(y-t)^2\)</span>, which quantity is treated as a constant when differentiating with respect to \(y\)?</p>
          <p>2. Why can we rewrite \(\sigma^\prime(z)\) as \(y(1-y)\)?</p>
          <p>3. Why is <span class="mono">\(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_1}=x_1\)</span>?</p>
          <p>4. Which of the three local derivatives tells us how the loss responds to the prediction? Which tells us how the activation responds to its input?</p>
        </div>
      </details>
    </div>

  <section id="panel-learn" class="section hidden space">
    <div class="card">
      <div class="small muted">Section 3</div>
      <div class="h2">Gradient descent uses the gradients from backpropagation</div>
      <p>Backpropagation tells us how the loss changes when each parameter changes. Gradient descent uses that information to decide how to update the parameters so that the loss becomes smaller.</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[w_{\mathrm{new}}=w_{\mathrm{old}}-\alpha\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\]</div>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">What does the update rule mean?</div>
      <p class="small">Focus on one weight first. Suppose the derivative \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\) is positive. That means increasing <span class="mono">w</span> would increase the loss locally, so we should move <span class="mono">w</span> in the opposite direction.</p>
      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>If \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}>0\)</strong>
          <p>Increasing <span class="mono">w</span> increases the loss, so gradient descent decreases <span class="mono">w</span>.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>If \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}<0\)</strong>
          <p>Increasing <span class="mono">w</span> decreases the loss, so gradient descent increases <span class="mono">w</span>.</p>
        </div>
      </div>
      <p class="small">This is why the update uses a minus sign: we move in the direction opposite the gradient.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">The role of the learning rate \(\alpha\)</div>
      <p class="small">The number \(\alpha\) controls how large the update is.</p>
      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card"><strong>Small \(\alpha\)</strong><p>Updates are cautious. Learning may be slow but stable.</p></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Moderate \(\alpha\)</strong><p>The network may reduce the loss efficiently.</p></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Large \(\alpha\)</strong><p>Updates may jump too far and can make the loss behave erratically.</p></div>
      </div>
      <p class="small">In this lesson, you will change \(\alpha\) yourself and observe how the training behavior changes.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">The full training loop</div>
      <div class="grid five small center">
        <div class="card">1. Forward pass</div>
        <div class="card">2. Compute loss</div>
        <div class="card">3. Backpropagate</div>
        <div class="card">4. Update parameters</div>
        <div class="card">5. Repeat</div>
      </div>
      <p class="small">A neural network learns by repeating this cycle many times. Each cycle uses the current weights, measures the current error, computes the gradients, and then adjusts the parameters.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Before you press the update button</div>
      <p class="small">For the experiment below, the input is fixed at \(x_1=1,

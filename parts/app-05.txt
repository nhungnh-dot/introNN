    <div class="card">
      <div class="small muted">Section 4</div>
      <div class="h2">Why can training end at different weights?</div>
      <p>Gradient descent starts from the initial weights and keeps updating them to reduce the loss.</p>
      <div class="card center" style="font-size:1.08em">
        <strong>Different starting weights can follow different paths and finish at different solutions.</strong>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Step 1 · What does the single data point tell us?</div>
      <p class="small">Use the neuron</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2).
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">For the one training point \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\), the prediction becomes</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1+w_2).
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">The target is chosen to be \(t=\sigma(1)\). Therefore, a perfect fit only requires</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell" style="font-size:1.15em">\[
        \boxed{w_1+w_2=1}.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        The data tell us the <strong>sum</strong> of the weights, but not the individual values of \(w_1\) and \(w_2\).
      </div>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card">\((1,0)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
        <div class="card">\((0.5,0.5)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
        <div class="card">\((2,-1)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
      </div>
      <p class="small center"><strong>All of these are valid final solutions.</strong></p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Step 2 · The starting points are not solutions</div>
      <p class="small">Now start gradient descent from three different places:</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">

      </div>

    <details class="card">
      <summary>Why do the three runs land at different places?</summary>
      <div class="small">
        <p>For this one-point problem, the loss depends only on \(w_1+w_2\). Therefore,</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}
          =
          \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}.
        \]</div>

        <p>Both weights change by exactly the same amount at every update. Therefore,</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_1-w_2
          \quad\text{stays constant during training.}
        \]</div>

        <p>So each starting point keeps its original difference \(w_1-w_2\), while gradient descent changes the sum until \(w_1+w_2=1\). That selects a different final point for each initialization.</p>
      </div>
    </details>

        <div class="h3">Before reading the table: what does the loss mean?</div>
        <p class="small">For each training point, the network makes a prediction \(y\) and compares it with the target \(t\) using the squared-error loss</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          L=(y-t)^2.
        \]</div>

        <p class="small">If we use only one training point, this is the loss shown for that point.</p>

        <p class="small">If we use several training points, we compute the squared error for each one and average them:</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          L_{\text{avg}}
          =
          \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(y_i-t_i\bigr)^2.
        \]</div>

        <div class="card small">
          <strong>When you read the results table, compare two things:</strong>
          <ol>
            <li>Did the three runs end with the same or different weights?</li>
            <li>Are their final losses all small?</li>
          </ol>
          If different final weights give similarly small losses, then the training data do not uniquely determine the parameters.

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 4 · Why is having more information helpful?</div>

      <p class="small">With only one training point, the network does not have enough information to determine both weights separately.</p>

      <p class="small">For the point \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\), a perfect fit only tells us</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1+w_2=1.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">This is one equation for two unknowns, so there are infinitely many possible weight pairs.</p>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Think of it this way:</strong> one data point asks the network only one question about the weights. It learns the combination \(w_1+w_2\), but it cannot tell how much of that value came from \(w_1\) and how much came from \(w_2\).
      </div>

      <p class="small">Now add a second training point with</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(1,0).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">For this point, the prediction becomes</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">So this observation gives information about \(w_1\) by itself. It is asking a different question than the first point.</p>

      <p class="small">Next add another point,</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(0,1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Now the prediction is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_2),
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">so this point gives information about \(w_2\) by itself.</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 1: \((1,1)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives information about the combination \(w_1+w_2\).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 2: \((1,0)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives direct information about \(w_1\).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 3: \((0,1)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives direct information about \(w_2\).</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Each new point adds another constraint</div>
        <p class="small">A weight pair must now fit <strong>all</strong> of the training points at the same time.</p>

        <div class="grid cards3 small center">
          <div class="card">
            <strong>1 point</strong><br><br>
            many possible<br>weight pairs
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>More varied points</strong><br><br>
            fewer weight pairs<br>fit everything
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Enough information</strong><br><br>
            the possible weights<br>can become tightly constrained
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>Geometric picture:</strong> each informative data point adds a new condition on \((w_1,w_2)\). With one condition, we had an entire line of possible solutions. Additional independent conditions can intersect that line and shrink the set of possible solutions dramatically.
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Why varied data matter more than repeated data</div>
        <p class="small">Suppose we repeat the same point \((1,1)\) many times. We are still learning essentially the same condition,</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_1+w_2\approx1.
        \]</div>
        <p class="small">Repeating that observation can reinforce the same information, but it does not tell us how to separate \(w_1\) from \(w_2\).</p>

        <p class="small">By contrast, using different input combinations such as \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,-1)\), or \((2,1)\) makes the network observe the weights from different directions.</p>

        <div class="card panel small">
          <strong>This is why “more data” really means more useful information.</strong> A large dataset is most helpful when the examples are varied enough to constrain different aspects of the model.
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">What should happen in our experiment?</div>
        <p class="small">With only one point, the three initializations can reach different final weights while still achieving very small loss.</p>
        <p class="small">With seven varied points, the weights must satisfy several conditions at once. That gives gradient descent much less freedom, so the three runs should end much closer together.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 5 · What changes in the second experiment?</div>
      <p class="small">When you switch from 1 point to 7 varied points, compare the three runs again.</p>

      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 1 point</strong>
          <p>There is a whole line of equally good solutions. Different initializations can end at different places on that line.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 7 varied points</strong>
          <p>The extra observations provide more independent constraints, so the runs are pulled toward nearly the same weights.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Big picture:</strong> initialization decides where training begins. The data determine which solutions are compatible with what we observed. More informative data reduce the number of plausible solutions.
      </div>
    </div>

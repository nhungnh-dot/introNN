  <section id="panel-bigpicture" class="section hidden space">
    <div class="card">
      <div class="small muted">Section 4</div>
      <div class="h2">Why can training end at different weights?</div>
      <p>Gradient descent starts from the initial weights and keeps updating them to reduce the loss.</p>
      <div class="card center" style="font-size:1.08em">
        <strong>Different starting weights can follow different paths and finish at different solutions.</strong>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Step 1 · What does the single training example tell us?</div>
      <p class="small">In supervised learning, each training example contains both the <strong>input</strong> and the <strong>desired output</strong>. Here we use</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell" style="font-size:1.08em">\[
        \boxed{(x_1,x_2,t)=(1,1,\sigma(1))}.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        Here, \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\) is the input and \(t=\sigma(1)\) is the target value that the network should learn to predict for this input. Numerically, \(\sigma(1)\approx0.731\).
      </div>

      <p class="small">The neuron is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">For this training input, the prediction becomes</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1+w_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">So a perfect fit means the prediction must equal the target:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \sigma(w_1+w_2)=\sigma(1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Since the sigmoid is one-to-one, this gives</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell" style="font-size:1.15em">\[
        \boxed{w_1+w_2=1}.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        The data tell us the <strong>sum</strong> of the weights, but not the individual values of \(w_1\) and \(w_2\).
      </div>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card">\((1,0)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
        <div class="card">\((0.5,0.5)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
        <div class="card">\((2,-1)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 1</span></div>
      </div>
      <p class="small center"><strong>All of these are valid final solutions.</strong></p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Step 2 · The starting points are not solutions</div>
      <p class="small">Now start gradient descent from three different places:</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card"><strong>Start A</strong><br>\((-3,3)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 0</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Start B</strong><br>\((3,3)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 6</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Start C</strong><br>\((3,-3)\)<br><span class="muted">sum = 0</span></div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>These are only initial weights.</strong> None of them satisfies \(w_1+w_2=1\), so gradient descent must move them.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Step 3 · Start → train → finish</div>
      <p class="small">Each run moves until it reaches a point that fits the data.</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Run A</strong><br><br>
          \((-3,3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">start</span><br><br>
          ↓ gradient descent ↓<br><br>
          \((-2.5,3.5)\)<br>
          <strong>finish</strong><br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 1</span>
        </div>

        <div class="card">
          <strong>Run B</strong><br><br>
          \((3,3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">start</span><br><br>
          ↓ gradient descent ↓<br><br>
          \((0.5,0.5)\)<br>
          <strong>finish</strong><br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 1</span>
        </div>

        <div class="card">
          <strong>Run C</strong><br><br>
          \((3,-3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">start</span><br><br>
          ↓ gradient descent ↓<br><br>
          \((3.5,-2.5)\)<br>
          <strong>finish</strong><br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 1</span>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Step 4 · Why do we get these particular finished weights?</div>
        <p class="small">For the one-point problem, two facts determine the final weights.</p>

        <div class="card small center">
          <strong>Fact 1: the final weights must fit the training data</strong><br><br>
          For the training example \((x_1,x_2,t)=(1,1,\sigma(1))\), a perfect fit requires
          \[
            \boxed{w_1+w_2=1}.
          \]
        </div>

        <div class="card panel" style="margin-top:12px">
          <div class="h3">Fact 2 · Why does gradient descent keep \(w_1-w_2\) unchanged?</div>

          <p class="small">Recall the gradient descent rule from Section 3:</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            w_1^{\text{new}}
            =
            w_1^{\text{old}}
            -\alpha\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1},
            \qquad
            w_2^{\text{new}}
            =
            w_2^{\text{old}}
            -\alpha\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}.
          \]</div>

          <p class="small">For our training input \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\), both weights enter the neuron in exactly the same way:</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            y=\sigma(w_1+w_2).
          \]</div>

          <p class="small">Therefore the loss changes by the same amount when we change \(w_1\) or \(w_2\):</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}
            =
            \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}.
          \]</div>

          <p class="small">Call this common gradient \(g\). One gradient descent step is then</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            w_1^{\text{new}}=w_1^{\text{old}}-\alpha g,
            \qquad
            w_2^{\text{new}}=w_2^{\text{old}}-\alpha g.
          \]</div>

          <p class="small"><strong>Both weights move by exactly the same amount.</strong> For example, if gradient descent subtracts \(0.2\), then</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            (w_1,w_2)
            \longrightarrow
            (w_1-0.2,\;w_2-0.2).
          \]</div>

          <p class="small">Subtract the two new weights:</p>
          <div class="card center math-cell">\[
            \begin{aligned}
            w_1^{\text{new}}-w_2^{\text{new}}
            &=(w_1^{\text{old}}-\alpha g)
              -(w_2^{\text{old}}-\alpha g)\\
            &=w_1^{\text{old}}-w_2^{\text{old}}.
            \end{aligned}
          \]</div>

          <div class="card center small" style="margin-top:10px">
            <strong>The \(-\alpha g\) terms cancel.</strong><br>
            Therefore every gradient descent step preserves
            \[
              \boxed{w_1-w_2}.
            \]
          </div>
        </div>

        <p class="small">So for each run, we solve two equations: one for the final sum and one for the preserved difference.</p>

        <div class="card small">
          <strong>Work through Run A</strong><br><br>
          Start: \((-3,3)\), so the preserved difference is
          \[
            w_1-w_2=-6.
          \]
          At the finish, we also need
          \[
            w_1+w_2=1.
          \]
          Therefore,
          \[
            \begin{cases}
            w_1+w_2=1,\\
            w_1-w_2=-6.
            \end{cases}
          \]
          Adding the equations gives
          \[
            2w_1=-5
            \quad\Rightarrow\quad
            w_1=-2.5.
          \]
          Then
          \[
            w_2=3.5.
          \]
          So Run A finishes at
          \[
            \boxed{(-2.5,3.5)}.
          \]
        </div>

        <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
          <strong>Your turn:</strong> Repeat the same two-equation idea for Runs B and C. First compute the starting value of \(w_1-w_2\), then combine it with \(w_1+w_2=1\). Check that you obtain the final weights below.
        </div>

        <div class="grid cards2 small center" style="margin-top:12px">
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Run B</strong><br><br>
            Start: \((3,3)\)<br><br>
            Verify that the finish is
            \[
              \boxed{(0.5,0.5)}.
            \]
          </div>

          <div class="card">
            <strong>Run C</strong><br><br>
            Start: \((3,-3)\)<br><br>
            Verify that the finish is
            \[
              \boxed{(3.5,-2.5)}.
            \]
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
          <strong>So the final point is not arbitrary.</strong> Gradient descent moves each run until it fits the data, while preserving the difference between the two weights that came from the initialization.
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>Main observation:</strong> all three final points fit the same training point perfectly, but the final weights are different.
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Now evaluate the three trained networks on a new input</div>
        <p class="small">So far, all three runs have been judged only on the training point \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\). On that point, all three final weight pairs satisfy \(w_1+w_2=1\), so they make the same prediction and have essentially zero training loss.</p>

        <p class="small">Now give the trained networks a new input:</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          (x_1,x_2)=(1,0).
        \]</div>

        <p class="small">For this new input,</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          y=\sigma(w_1\cdot1+w_2\cdot0)=\sigma(w_1).
        \]</div>

        <p class="small">Therefore, the three trained networks now make very different predictions:</p>

        <div class="grid cards3 small center">
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Run A</strong><br>
            final weights \((-2.5,3.5)\)<br><br>
            \[
              y=\sigma(-2.5)\approx0.076
            \]
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Run B</strong><br>
            final weights \((0.5,0.5)\)<br><br>
            \[
              y=\sigma(0.5)\approx0.622
            \]
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Run C</strong><br>
            final weights \((3.5,-2.5)\)<br><br>
            \[
              y=\sigma(3.5)\approx0.971
            \]
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
          <strong>This is the important lesson:</strong> the three models fit the original training point equally well, but they behave very differently on a new input. So achieving a very small training loss does not by itself tell us that the model has learned the underlying relationship well.
        </div>

        <p class="small">This is why more varied training data are useful: they test the model at more input combinations and reduce the freedom for very different networks to fit the training data while making very different predictions elsewhere.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

        <div class="h3">Before reading the table: what does the loss mean?</div>
        <p class="small">For each training point, the network makes a prediction \(y\) and compares it with the target \(t\) using the squared-error loss</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          L=(y-t)^2.
        \]</div>

        <p class="small">If we use only one training point, this is the loss shown for that point.</p>

        <p class="small">If we use several training points, we compute the squared error for each one and average them:</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          L_{\text{avg}}
          =
          \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(y_i-t_i\bigr)^2.
        \]</div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 4 · Why is having more information helpful?</div>

      <p class="small">With only one training point, the network does not have enough information to determine both weights separately.</p>

      <p class="small">For the point \((x_1,x_2)=(1,1)\), a perfect fit only tells us</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1+w_2=1.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">This is one equation for two unknowns, so there are infinitely many possible weight pairs.</p>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Think of it this way:</strong> one data point asks the network only one question about the weights. It learns the combination \(w_1+w_2\), but it cannot tell how much of that value came from \(w_1\) and how much came from \(w_2\).
      </div>

      <p class="small">Now add a second training point with</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(1,0).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">For this point, the prediction becomes</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">So this observation gives information about \(w_1\) by itself. It is asking a different question than the first point.</p>

      <p class="small">Next add another point,</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(0,1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Now the prediction is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_2),
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">so this point gives information about \(w_2\) by itself.</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 1: \((1,1)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives information about the combination \(w_1+w_2\).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 2: \((1,0)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives direct information about \(w_1\).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Point 3: \((0,1)\)</strong>
          <p>Gives direct information about \(w_2\).</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Each new point adds another constraint</div>
        <p class="small">A weight pair must now fit <strong>all</strong> of the training points at the same time.</p>

        <div class="grid cards3 small center">
          <div class="card">
            <strong>1 point</strong><br><br>
            many possible<br>weight pairs
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>More varied points</strong><br><br>
            fewer weight pairs<br>fit everything
          </div>
          <div class="card">
            <strong>Enough information</strong><br><br>
            the possible weights<br>can become tightly constrained
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>Geometric picture:</strong> each informative data point adds a new condition on \((w_1,w_2)\). With one condition, we had an entire line of possible solutions. Additional independent conditions can intersect that line and shrink the set of possible solutions dramatically.
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">Why varied data matter more than repeated data</div>
        <p class="small">Suppose we repeat the same point \((1,1)\) many times. We are still learning essentially the same condition,</p>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_1+w_2\approx1.
        \]</div>
        <p class="small">Repeating that observation can reinforce the same information, but it does not tell us how to separate \(w_1\) from \(w_2\).</p>

        <p class="small">By contrast, using different input combinations such as \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,-1)\), or \((2,1)\) makes the network observe the weights from different directions.</p>

        <div class="card panel small">
          <strong>This is why “more data” really means more useful information.</strong> A large dataset is most helpful when the examples are varied enough to constrain different aspects of the model.
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="h3">What should happen in our experiment?</div>
        <p class="small">With only one point, the three initializations can reach different final weights while still achieving very small loss.</p>
        <p class="small">With seven varied points, the weights must satisfy several conditions at once. That gives gradient descent much less freedom, so the three runs should end much closer together.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 5 · What changes in the second experiment?</div>
      <p class="small">When you switch from 1 point to 7 varied points, compare the three runs again.</p>

      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 1 point</strong>
          <p>There is a whole line of equally good solutions. Different initializations can end at different places on that line.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 7 varied points</strong>
          <p>The extra observations provide more independent constraints, so the runs are pulled toward nearly the same weights.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Big picture:</strong> initialization decides where training begins. The data determine which solutions are compatible with what we observed. More informative data reduce the number of plausible solutions.
      </div>
    </div>

      <p class="small">A convolutional neural network, or CNN, is designed to take advantage of the spatial structure of those pixels. Instead of connecting every pixel to every neuron immediately, a CNN first looks at small local regions and asks whether certain visual patterns are present.</p>

  <section id="panel-bigpicture" class="section hidden space">
    <div class="card">
      <div class="small muted">Section 4</div>
      <div class="h2">The big picture: why initialization and data matter</div>
      <p>So far, we have focused on <strong>how</strong> a neural network learns: forward pass, backpropagation, then gradient descent. Now we zoom out and ask a different question:</p>
      <div class="card center" style="font-size:1.05em"><strong>If two networks use the same training rule, will they always learn the same weights?</strong></div>
      <p class="small">Not necessarily. The answer depends on both <strong>where the weights start</strong> and <strong>how much useful information the data provide</strong>.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 1 · Why can the starting weights matter?</div>
      <p class="small">Consider the same two-weight neuron:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Suppose we have only one training example,</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(1,1),
        \qquad
        t=\sigma(1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">For this single point, the neuron predicts</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1+w_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">To match the target exactly, we need</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \sigma(w_1+w_2)=\sigma(1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Because the sigmoid is one-to-one, this means</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \boxed{w_1+w_2=1}.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Important:</strong> this is not one solution. It is an entire line of solutions in the \((w_1,w_2)\)-plane. For example,
        \[
          (1,0),\quad (0,1),\quad (2,-1)
        \]
        all give the same prediction for this one training point.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 2 · What does gradient descent do on this one-point problem?</div>
      <p class="small">For this dataset, the loss is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        L(w_1,w_2)=\bigl(\sigma(w_1+w_2)-\sigma(1)\bigr)^2.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Notice that \(L\) depends on the weights only through the combination \(w_1+w_2\). Therefore,</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}
        =
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Call this common derivative \(g\). One gradient-descent step gives</p>
      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_1^{\text{new}}=w_1-\alpha g
        \]</div>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_2^{\text{new}}=w_2-\alpha g.
        \]</div>
      </div>

      <p class="small">Now subtract the two updated weights:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1^{\text{new}}-w_2^{\text{new}}
        =
        w_1-w_2.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>This is the key.</strong> The difference \(w_1-w_2\) stays unchanged during training. So the initialization determines which point on the line \(w_1+w_2=1\) the algorithm approaches.
      </div>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card"><strong>Start A</strong><br>\((w_1,w_2)=(-3,3)\)<br><span class="muted">difference = -6</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Start B</strong><br>\((w_1,w_2)=(3,3)\)<br><span class="muted">difference = 0</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Start C</strong><br>\((w_1,w_2)=(3,-3)\)<br><span class="muted">difference = 6</span></div>
      </div>

      <p class="small"><strong>Before running the experiment:</strong> do you expect these three runs to reach the same final weights, or three different points on the same solution line?</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 3 · Experiment: same model, different starting weights</div>
      <p class="small">Run the experiment twice. First use only one training point. Then switch to seven varied points and compare what changes.</p>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 4 · Why does adding more data help?</div>
      <p class="small">One training point gave us only one condition:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1+w_2=1.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Now suppose we also observe a point such as \((x_1,x_2)=(1,0)\). Its prediction depends mostly on \(w_1\). A point such as \((0,1)\) gives direct information about \(w_2\). Other input directions add still more independent information.</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>One point</strong>
          <p>Many weight pairs can behave identically on that one observation.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>More varied points</strong>
          <p>Different inputs probe the weights in different directions.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Result</strong>
          <p>The set of weight pairs that fit all observations becomes much smaller.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Geometric picture:</strong> with one data point, the loss has a long flat valley of equally good solutions. Different starts can settle at different places along that valley. With sufficiently informative data, that flat direction is reduced and the loss looks more like a well-defined basin.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 5 · More data is useful only if it adds information</div>
      <p class="small">Saying “we need lots of data” is useful, but there is an important refinement:</p>
      <div class="card center"><strong>We need enough <em>informative and varied</em> data.</strong></div>

      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Not very helpful</strong>
          <p>Repeating the same training point 100 times gives essentially the same constraint 100 times.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Much more helpful</strong>
          <p>Observations with different input combinations can constrain different directions in parameter space.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <p class="small">In large neural networks, the situation is more complicated because there can be many parameters, many local minima or flat directions, and many different parameter choices that produce similar predictions. This is one reason modern neural networks are usually trained with large, diverse datasets.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Connect the whole lesson</div>
      <div class="grid cards4 small center">
        <div class="card"><strong>Forward pass</strong><br><span class="muted">make a prediction</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Backpropagation</strong><br><span class="muted">compute gradients</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Gradient descent</strong><br><span class="muted">update parameters</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Data + initialization</strong><br><span class="muted">shape where learning can go</span></div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>Big idea:</strong> training is not determined by the optimization rule alone. The learned model depends on the <strong>initial parameters</strong>, the <strong>training data</strong>, and the <strong>loss landscape</strong>. More informative data can reduce ambiguity, but it does not automatically guarantee a unique solution.
      </div>
    </div>

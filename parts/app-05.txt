  <section id="panel-bigpicture" class="section hidden space">
    <div class="card">
      <div class="small muted">Section 4</div>
      <div class="h2">The big picture: why initialization and data matter</div>
      <p>We now know how a neural network trains: make a prediction, compute gradients, and update the weights.</p>
      <p>But there is one more important question:</p>
      <div class="card center" style="font-size:1.05em"><strong>If we start with different weights, do we always end with the same trained network?</strong></div>
      <p class="small">Not always. This section shows why.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 1 · One data point does not determine both weights</div>
      <p class="small">Use the same neuron:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Suppose our only training example is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(1,1),
        \qquad
        t=\sigma(1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Then the network prediction is</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        y=\sigma(w_1+w_2).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">To match the target exactly, we need</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \sigma(w_1+w_2)=\sigma(1).
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Since the sigmoid is one-to-one, this means</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \boxed{w_1+w_2=1}.
      \]</div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>This is the first important idea:</strong> the data tell us the <em>sum</em> of the two weights, but they do not tell us how that total should be split between \(w_1\) and \(w_2\).
      </div>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card">\((w_1,w_2)=(1,0)\)</div>
        <div class="card">\((w_1,w_2)=(0.5,0.5)\)</div>
        <div class="card">\((w_1,w_2)=(2,-1)\)</div>
      </div>
      <p class="small center">All three satisfy \(w_1+w_2=1\), so all three fit this one training point perfectly.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 2 · Different starting points can lead to different final weights</div>
      <p class="small">Now suppose we start training from one of these points:</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start A</strong><br>
          \((-3,3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 0</span>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start B</strong><br>
          \((3,3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 6</span>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start C</strong><br>
          \((3,-3)\)<br>
          <span class="muted">sum = 0</span>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Notice:</strong> these are only <em>starting points</em>. They are not solutions yet because their sums are not equal to 1.
      </div>

      <p class="small">Gradient descent changes the weights so that the sum moves toward 1.</p>

      <p class="small">For this one-point problem, the loss depends only on \(w_1+w_2\), so</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}
        =
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">That means each gradient-descent step changes the two weights by the same amount:</p>
      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_1^{\text{new}}=w_1-\alpha g
        \]</div>
        <div class="card center math-cell">\[
          w_2^{\text{new}}=w_2-\alpha g.
        \]</div>
      </div>

      <p class="small">So their difference does not change:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1^{\text{new}}-w_2^{\text{new}}
        =
        w_1-w_2.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">Now we can predict where each run will finish. At the end, two things must be true:</p>
      <div class="grid cards2 small center">
        <div class="card"><strong>Fit the data</strong><br>\(w_1+w_2=1\)</div>
        <div class="card"><strong>Keep the starting difference</strong><br>\(w_1-w_2=\text{constant}\)</div>
      </div>

      <div class="grid cards3 small center" style="margin-top:12px">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start A</strong><br>
          \((-3,3)\)<br>
          \(w_1-w_2=-6\)<br><br>
          <strong>↓ training ↓</strong><br><br>
          <strong>Final</strong><br>
          \((-2.5,3.5)\)
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start B</strong><br>
          \((3,3)\)<br>
          \(w_1-w_2=0\)<br><br>
          <strong>↓ training ↓</strong><br><br>
          <strong>Final</strong><br>
          \((0.5,0.5)\)
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Start C</strong><br>
          \((3,-3)\)<br>
          \(w_1-w_2=6\)<br><br>
          <strong>↓ training ↓</strong><br><br>
          <strong>Final</strong><br>
          \((3.5,-2.5)\)
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small" style="margin-top:12px">
        <strong>Key idea:</strong> the three runs start at different points, move during training, and finally reach <strong>different points on the same solution line</strong> \(w_1+w_2=1\).
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Part 4 · Why do more data help?</div>
      <p class="small">With only one point, the network only learns</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        w_1+w_2=1.
      \]</div>

      <p class="small">That is not enough to determine \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) separately.</p>

      <p class="small">Now imagine adding a point with</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(1,0).
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">For this point, the prediction depends on \(w_1\) but not on \(w_2\). This gives us new information about \(w_1\).</p>

      <p class="small">Another point such as</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[
        (x_1,x_2)=(0,1)
      \]</div>
      <p class="small">gives new information about \(w_2\).</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>One point</strong>
          <p>Only constrains one combination, such as \(w_1+w_2\).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>Different points</strong>
          <p>Probe the weights in different ways.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>More information</strong>
          <p>Makes it harder for many very different weight pairs to fit all the data.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>So the lesson is not just “use more data.”</strong> We want enough <strong>varied and informative data</strong> to constrain the model.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Part 5 · What changes in the second experiment?</div>
      <p class="small">When you switch from 1 point to 7 varied points, compare the three runs again.</p>

      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 1 point</strong>
          <p>There is a whole line of equally good solutions. Different initializations can end at different places on that line.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>With 7 varied points</strong>
          <p>The extra observations provide more independent constraints, so the runs are pulled toward nearly the same weights.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Big picture:</strong> initialization decides where training begins. The data determine which solutions are compatible with what we observed. More informative data reduce the number of plausible solutions.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card panel">
      <div class="h3">Connect the whole lesson</div>
      <div class="grid cards4 small center">
        <div class="card"><strong>1. Forward pass</strong><br><span class="muted">make a prediction</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>2. Backpropagation</strong><br><span class="muted">compute gradients</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>3. Gradient descent</strong><br><span class="muted">update the weights</span></div>
        <div class="card"><strong>4. Data + initialization</strong><br><span class="muted">influence the final model</span></div>
      </div>
    </div>
